# Brief — Media Probe · 2/5 Critical Decisions

Nine decisions. **PROPOSED — none of this exists yet** unless a pin is quoted.
Decisions 1, 3 and 4 are the ones that determine whether this tool is worth
building; the rest are "reuse what works, and say so".

---

## Decision 1 — The instrument is the deliverable; the recommender is a by-product

**Decide:** ship the plots and distributions first, and treat `plan()` as a thin
layer over numbers a human can already see.

**Why:** the failure this pack responds to was not a bad threshold, it was an
*invisible* one. If we build the recommender first, we replace one opaque
heuristic with a cleverer opaque heuristic and lose the ability to notice the next
time it is wrong. Every number `plan()` uses must be visible on screen before
`plan()` is allowed to use it.

**Test of compliance:** for any boundary `plan()` proposes, the UI can show the
evidence — the energy trace, the gap that qualified, the frame diff that fired.
No boundary may exist without a visible reason.

---

## Decision 2 — No model calls, ever, in this tool

**Decide:** the probe is pure signal processing. Zero OpenRouter usage.

**Why:** its whole value is being free to run *before* you spend $0.43 on an
import. A probe that itself costs money would not get used on the recording you
are unsure about — which is precisely the recording that needs it. It also keeps
the privacy story trivial: nothing leaves the browser, no key required.

---

## Decision 3 — Compute FOUR frame-difference metrics, and let real recordings pick the winner

**Decide:** do not choose a metric from first principles. Compute all four on every
probe and plot them on the same axis.

| Metric | Sees | Blind to |
|---|---|---|
| **meanAbs** — mean absolute greyscale difference (what `nr-frames` uses today, pin: `tools/v0/v0.1/v0.1.66/en-gb/narrated-review/api/nr-frames.js`) | global change | a dialog opening in one corner (averaged away) |
| **blockMax** — max meanAbs over an 8×8 grid of tiles | *localised* change: a panel, a menu, a modal | slow global drift |
| **edgeDiff** — difference of gradient magnitude (Sobel-ish) | layout/structure change | recolouring, theme switch |
| **histDist** — distance between greyscale histograms | palette/brightness change | content moving without changing its colours |

**Why:** the current single metric is exactly the kind of unexamined choice that
produced `silenceThreshold: 0.01`. `meanAbs` is known to be wrong in both
directions for screencasts: a full-screen brightness change reads as huge, and a
new dialog over a mostly-unchanged page reads as tiny. Four cheap metrics on a
32×18 raster cost almost nothing, and seeing where they *disagree* is the useful
signal — disagreement is what a scroll, a fade or a cursor looks like.

**What we are buying:** the answer to "which metric separates *the screen changed*
from *the cursor moved* on Dinis's actual recordings". That is an empirical
question and it has never been asked.

## Decision 4 — For audio, the deliverable is a DISTRIBUTION, not a threshold

**Decide:** the primary audio output is a histogram of framewise energy with the
noise-floor mode and the speech mode marked, plus a histogram of gap lengths at
several candidate thresholds *simultaneously*.

**Why:** this is the direct lesson of the failure. The bug was not "0.01 is too
low", it was "we compared an absolute number against an unknown distribution". A
histogram makes the bug a picture:

```
   BROKEN recording                        HEALTHY recording
   ▁▂▅███▅▂▁▁▂▃▂▁                          ██▅▂▁    ▁▂▄▅▄▂▁
       ▲    ▲                              ▲            ▲
    floor  speech                        floor        speech
    (0.04) (0.09)                        (0.002)      (0.08)
       └── 0.01 is BELOW the floor ──┘      └─ 0.01 separates them ─┘
```

Alongside it, the gap histogram answers the question that actually decides the
strategy:

| Gap population | Typical length | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| word gaps | ~100–150 ms | never a boundary |
| sentence gaps | ~400–700 ms | maybe a boundary |
| topic gaps | > 1000 ms | a boundary worth having |

**If the >1000 ms population is empty, audio-led segmentation cannot work on this
recording** — and you know it in one second, for free. That is the single most
valuable output in the whole pack.

**Also compute spectral flatness per frame.** Room tone and mains hum are narrow
band (very low flatness); speech is broadband. Flatness distinguishes "quiet but
noisy" from "quiet and empty", which pure RMS cannot — and "quiet but noisy" is
exactly what defeated us.

## Decision 5 — Browser-native first; FFmpeg only for what only FFmpeg can do

**Decide:** implement both lanes, default to browser-native, and use the probe to
*measure* whether FFmpeg's answers differ enough to justify its cost.

Browser-native (a `<video>` + canvas + `decodeAudioData`) already works and is
what `narrated-review` uses today. FFmpeg WASM is a multi-megabyte CDN load and a
slow decode, so it must earn its place. It genuinely does for:

| Need | Why only FFmpeg |
|---|---|
| **Codecs the browser refuses** | HEVC `.mov` cannot be decoded in a `<video>` on Chrome/Firefox on Linux/Windows (pin: `core/sg-video-frames/v0/v0.1/v0.1.0/sg-video-frames.js` documents this) |
| **`select='gt(scene,N)'` + `showinfo`** | a scene score per frame at *native* frame rate, not at our sampling rate |
| **`silencedetect=n=-30dB:d=0.5`** | silence intervals computed on the full-rate signal |
| **`astats` / `ebur128`** | proper loudness measurement (LUFS), not our hand-rolled RMS |
| Frame-accurate extraction at a timestamp | seeking a `<video>` lands on the nearest keyframe-decodable frame, not necessarily the exact one |

**The interesting experiment:** FFmpeg's `silencedetect` and `scene` filters do
*exactly* what we hand-rolled and got wrong. Running both and plotting them on the
same axis tells us whether our implementation is subtly broken or merely
differently tuned. That comparison is worth the WASM load once, even if the
shipped default never uses it.

**Non-negotiable:** the probe must be fully useful with FFmpeg switched off. It
loads on demand, per lane, never at boot.

---

## Decision 6 — Sampling rate is a parameter, and the cost of it must be shown

**Decide:** frame sampling defaults to ~4 fps (250 ms) with the interval exposed,
and the UI shows the wall-clock cost of the chosen rate before running.

**Why:** frame extraction is the slow part — one seek plus two canvas draws per
sample. A 60-minute recording at 250 ms is 14 400 seeks, which is minutes of
work, and users will pick a long video first. Two mitigations to build in:

1. **Two-pass:** a coarse pass (1 fps) to find candidate regions, then a fine pass
   (10 fps) only around detected changes. Most of a screencast is static.
2. Report progress per pass and make it cancellable. A probe that cannot be
   interrupted will not be run twice.

---

## Decision 7 — Measure the lead/lag; do not assume it

**Decide:** compute the distribution of `(nearest speech onset − scene change)`
across the recording and display it. `narrated-review`'s current lead 2500 ms /
lag 1200 ms window becomes a *measured* default per speaker.

**Why:** those two numbers are my guesses, written into
`nr-video.js` `VIDEO_DEFAULTS` on the reasoning that "the picture leads the
words". The reasoning is sound; the magnitudes are invented. A presenter who
narrates *then* clicks has a negative lead, and the video smoke's discriminating
case shows the search window has to cover that — but nobody knows the real shape.

The distribution also detects the pathological case: if scene changes and speech
onsets are *uncorrelated*, then this recording's pictures and words do not line up
at all, and any pairing will be arbitrary. Better to say so than to produce a
confident document.

---

## Decision 8 — `plan()` must carry its reasons, and refuse when it cannot

**Decide:** `plan()` returns, per boundary, the rule that fired and the evidence.
When no strategy fits, it returns `{ strategy: 'none', reason }` rather than a
best-effort answer.

**Why:** the v0.1.4 import produced nine confident-looking captures out of a
recording it had completely failed to segment. Silence about failure is the
defect; v0.1.5 added a `capped` count and a warning for exactly this. `plan()`
inherits that discipline from the start: an honest refusal is a better output than
a plausible fabrication, and it is the caller's job to decide what to do about it.

---

## Decision 9 — Where the code lives

**Decide:** `core/sg-media-analysis` (pure, no DOM beyond a canvas/`<video>` for
sampling) + a thin tool at `/en-gb/media-probe/` that visualises it.
`narrated-review` consumes the core module — **later**, behind an option, and only
after the numbers have been validated on real recordings.

**Why this extraction and not the last one:** the `core/sg-review-pairs`
extraction proposed in the v0.2.87 pack was correctly abandoned, because it
existed only to let a second copy of the same workflow share code. This one is
different: the analysis has *two genuinely different consumers* — an instrument a
human reads and a pipeline that acts on it — and neither is a copy of the other.
A shared module here prevents the tool and the pipeline from disagreeing about
what a "scene change" is, which is a real risk once both compute it.

**Order matters:** instrument first, validate, *then* re-pin `narrated-review`.
Extracting on day one would freeze today's guesses into a shared API.
